Before you begin — please read

Every SOP here is general caregiving information, not medical advice. Follow the instructions of the hospice nurse and medical team caring for your person — their guidance for that individual always comes first. If anything causes pain, or you see new bleeding, broken skin, or anything that worries you, stop and call the care team.

One procedure you will not find here: anything for swallowing difficulties. If the person in your care coughs, chokes, or struggles to swallow, do not improvise — ask the care team to guide you in person.

    Who we help — the community

    Professional care runs on schedules. The hours in between — the nights, the changes, the sitting-with — are carried by amateurs. This society exists for them, and for the people in their care.

    Seniors with no one left

    Elders whose spouse, family, and friends have all passed, and whose only remaining circle is the people who choose to show up — lodge brothers, church members, neighbors, old friends. Nobody should reach ninety with an empty room.

    Hospice, bedridden & terminally ill

    People in home hospice and people confined to bed need steady, gentle, unhurried hands-on help many times a day — changing, freshening, turning, repositioning — done with dignity, every single time.

    The caregivers themselves

    Most amateur caregivers were never taught any of this. They are learning at 2 a.m., at a bedside, alone. We share step-by-step procedures written by caregivers who have done the work, plus the fellowship of people who understand what the work costs and what it gives.

    What an amateur caregiver is — and is not

    We work alongside the medical and hospice team, never instead of them. The boundary below is the heart of this society, and every member agrees to it.

    We do

    • Keep the person clean, dry, comfortable, and treated with dignity
    • Provide presence and companionship — sitting with, listening, being there
    • Follow clear, written, step-by-step procedures (SOPs) for everyday care tasks
    • Give family caregivers respite so they can rest
    • Watch, notice changes, and report them promptly to the hospice or medical team
    • Communicate with the person throughout every task, at their pace

    We never

    • Make medical decisions or change a care plan
    • Give, adjust, or advise on medication
    • Perform nursing or clinical procedures
    • Handle the person’s money, accounts, or legal documents
    • Replace the hospice nurse, doctor, or any member of the care team
    • Continue a task that causes pain or distress — we stop and call the care team

    Everything on this site is general caregiving information, shared from experience. It is not medical advice. The hospice or medical team caring for your person always has the final word.

    About Us

    A fellowship of family and volunteer caregivers — for seniors who have no one else left, and for the ordinary people who show up for them anyway.

    His father is the third senior he has cared for in volunteer status. The other two were Freemason men in their nineties who had no one else — everyone else had passed — and only their fraternity brothers were left to help out and watch over them.

    The Society of Amateur Caregivers is the work of Phillipp Dismukes, a caregiver in exactly that position: caring for his father in home hospice, and remembering the two men before him. The Society exists so that the next person sitting at a bedside for the first time does not have to figure it all out alone — and so that no senior’s last chapter is spent with nobody in the room.

    We are amateurs in the original sense of the word: people who do this for love, not for pay — like amateur golfers, good at what we do and choosing to stay amateur, not training to become professionals. What we have to offer each other is presence, practical knowledge, and the promise that someone else understands.

    Support Line — schedule a talk

    Sometimes you do not need advice. You need a conversation — a friendly voice, a positive pick-me-up, and someone in your corner saying: you can do this, and you are not doing it alone. That is what our Support Line is for.

    Cheerleading, not counselling

    Book a short, friendly talk with a fellow amateur caregiver — a peer, not a professional. We listen, we encourage, and we help you face the next hour, the next change, the next night shift at the bedside.

    • A friendly conversation when the house feels too quiet
    • Encouragement before a hard day, a hard visit, or a hard task
    • A pep talk when you are tired and doubting yourself
    • Someone who understands caregiving because they do it too
    • A reminder of what you are doing well — because you are

    Please be clear on what this is — and is not. The Support Line is peer support and encouragement only. It is not medical advice, not therapy or counselling, and not a crisis line. Our volunteers cannot help with medication, symptoms, or care decisions — for those, talk to the hospice or medical team caring for your person.

    If you are in crisis, do not wait for a scheduled talk

    If you are thinking about harming yourself, or you feel you cannot keep yourself safe, please reach out right now:

    Call or text 988 in the US

    That is the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline — free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency services.
